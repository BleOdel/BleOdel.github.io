Meta publishes what it checks, but not how

Of the three stores, Meta is the only one whose material in this scan states the privacy and security criteria it reviews against. Its Virtual Reality Check (VRC) guidelines require a privacy policy that explains data collection, data use and deletion requests (VRC.Quest.Privacy.1-4), that the team and app "clear data protection checks" (Privacy.5), and that an app request only the permissions it needs (Security.2). Meta publishes the test plans it says it reviews against. It does not say who runs them, how the data protection checks work, or how often apps fail, and it notes that "outright rejections are rare": a failing app goes back as Changes Requested.

The permission check is the one requirement an independent source corroborates. In Road to VR's 2021 account of a developer who published a deliberately empty app on App Lab, App Lab review rejected apps that request permissions they do not use, but did not judge scope or quality ("no content curation", in the developer's words). The privacy-policy requirement also appears in Callstack's submission guide, but that guide points back to Meta's own documentation, so it is a restatement, not independent corroboration.

Review gates the first release, not later code

Meta's "After submission" page says that once an app is released, app binaries update immediately without review, while metadata changes take 1-2 business days to review. If that is current, the store's code review covers an app's first version only. This rests on that one first-party page: nothing else in the scan confirms or qualifies it, so it is reported as Meta's own statement, not an established fact about enforcement.

Apple gates sensors by entitlement and channel, not by review

For Vision Pro, the scan found Apple's controls on sensitive capabilities rather than its review process. Main-camera access, passthrough in screen capture and similar capabilities each need a managed entitlement plus a license file, and are available only to in-house or custom enterprise apps distributed through Apple Business Manager, never the public App Store. Passthrough capture also needs the user to press the system broadcast button every time. As presented in this session, Apple keeps these capabilities out of the consumer store altogether rather than vetting their use app by app; how consumer visionOS apps are reviewed was not covered by anything this scan found.

PICO: obligations on paper, no published criteria

PICO's developer terms prohibit malware, make developers solely responsible for disclosing the permissions an app needs, require breach notification within 24 hours, and let PICO remove any app at its discretion. Its FAQ says apps launch only "after passing the test" but gives no privacy or security criteria. The obligations are contractual; nothing read in this scan shows how, or whether, PICO checks them before release.

  • No Apple App Store Review Guidelines or visionOS consumer-review material surfaced from these searches.
  • No description of what Meta's "data protection checks" involve, or of any automated scanning of submitted binaries.
