Cyber-Physical Impact of VR Attacks

What happens when a cyberattack affects both a machine and the person inside the experience?

I conducted a 48-participant study examining the effects of GPU-based attacks in virtual reality and users’ responses to an in-headset security warning. Participants completed normal, attack-only and attack-with-warning conditions. Evaluation combined the Virtual Reality Sickness Questionnaire (VRSQ), an IDS questionnaire and timing data.

In-headset warning demo

The warning as participants saw it: rendered inside the scene rather than as a system dialog, so it can be read without removing the headset or leaving the session.

Two attacks, one goal

A GPU-based attack used a custom C++ malware (delivered as if via a post-exploitation tool such as Meterpreter) that overwhelmed the GPU with long-running OpenGL tasks, hidden from the user, at four configurable intensities. A network-based attack flooded the target with continuous ICMP echo requests, a simple denial-of-service pattern, to disrupt a collaborative VR session over Photon PUN2 networking. Both attacks share the same goal: degrade frame rate enough to cause visual discomfort, screen tearing, and eventually VR sickness. The participant study described below used the GPU-based attack.

Research methods

I combined system telemetry, questionnaires, behavioural timing, task-performance measures and thematic analysis. Forty-eight participants completed normal, attack-only and attack-with-warning conditions. Two groups of 24 experienced the attack conditions in opposite orders, allowing me to examine both system behaviour and users’ responses.

Warning design

I designed the warning using Bauer et al.’s Warning Design Guidelines (2013), adapting the risk-assessment approach to the GPU-based attack scenario. The interface communicated the risk in plain language, used a warning icon and a consistent visual hierarchy, and gave the user an action to take. I then used Cranor’s Human-in-the-Loop framework (2008) to structure the questionnaire evaluating attention, comprehension, ability to act, perceived timing and relevance.

Participants rated the warning’s clarity at a mean of 4.48 out of 5 and its timeliness at 4.29 out of 5. These are participant ratings, not objective measurements of detection time.

In the participant study, the warning appeared an average of 25.98 seconds after attack onset. Nineteen of 48 participants (39.6%) took the requested action. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating both model detection and users’ responses to warnings in immersive environments.

What the warning responses revealed

Twenty-nine of 48 participants ignored the warning. Thematic analysis identified task engagement in 12 of those participants’ responses and the experimental context in nine. These findings suggest that detecting a threat and displaying a warning do not guarantee that an immersed user will act on it.

Limitations and next steps

The study also identified opportunities to improve the warning design, including more prominent placement and future evaluation of audio or haptic feedback. The Meta Quest 2 setup did not provide eye-tracking data. Maintaining the Unity testbed highlighted the compatibility risks of upgrading active XR projects. These limitations inform the next stage of development and evaluation.
